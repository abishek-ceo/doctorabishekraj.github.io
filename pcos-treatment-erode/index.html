What is PCOS?

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a hormonal condition in which the ovaries do not release eggs regularly and the body makes more male hormones (androgens) than usual. Many women with PCOS also have insulin resistance, which makes it harder to control weight and blood sugar. The ovaries may show many small follicles (“cysts”) on ultrasound, but these are not true cysts and do not need to be removed.

PCOD or PCOS?

In everyday use in India, “PCOD” and “PCOS” are often used for the same problem. Doctors use the term PCOS. Whatever you call it, the approach is similar: find out how it affects you, and treat what troubles you most.

Signs and symptoms

  • irregular, infrequent or missed periods
  • acne and oily skin
  • excess hair on the face and body (hirsutism), or thinning hair on the scalp
  • weight gain, especially around the waist
  • dark, velvety patches of skin on the neck or underarms
  • difficulty getting pregnant
  • mood changes, low self-esteem or poor sleep

Not every woman with PCOS has every sign, and some women with PCOS have a normal weight.

How PCOS is diagnosed

There is no single test. Your doctor will ask about your periods and symptoms, examine you, and usually advise a pelvic ultrasound and blood tests for hormones, thyroid, blood sugar and cholesterol.

PCOS is diagnosed when at least two of these three are present, after other causes have been ruled out: irregular or absent ovulation, signs of high male hormones (in your symptoms or blood tests), and polycystic-looking ovaries on ultrasound. In teenagers the diagnosis is made more carefully, because irregular periods are common in the first years.

Treatment: tailored to what you want

Treatment depends on your goals: regular periods, clearer skin, less hair growth, weight control or pregnancy.

  1. Lifestyle firstFood, activity, sleep and weight are the foundation of PCOS care.
  2. Medicines to regulate your periodsHormonal treatment can bring regular periods and also helps acne and unwanted hair.
  3. Treatment for insulin resistance, when neededSome women benefit from medicines that improve how the body handles sugar.
  4. Skin and hair treatmentTargeted treatment for acne and unwanted hair growth.
  5. Help to conceiveOvulation-inducing medicines with monitoring, and IUI or other fertility treatment if needed. Read about fertility and IUI treatment.

Diet and lifestyle that help

If you are overweight, losing even 5 to 10% of your body weight can help bring back regular ovulation. Useful habits for everyone with PCOS:

  • about 150 minutes of brisk activity a week, such as walking, cycling or swimming
  • balanced meals with whole grains, pulses, vegetables, fruit and protein
  • less sugary drinks, sweets, refined flour and fried snacks
  • regular meal times and 7 to 8 hours of sleep
  • ways to manage stress, such as walking, yoga or talking with family
  • no crash diets and no unproven “PCOS cures”

PCOS and pregnancy

PCOS can make it harder to conceive, but most women with PCOS can become pregnant, often with simple treatment such as ovulation-inducing medicines and monitoring. Over 500 conceptions through IUI have been achieved under Dr. Jayanthi Prabakaran’s care.

A pregnancy with PCOS has a higher chance of gestational diabetes, high blood pressure and early delivery, so early antenatal care matters. See our high-risk pregnancy care and maternity care.

Looking after your long-term health

PCOS is lifelong, but good care lowers the chance of problems later: type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and cholesterol, fatty liver, sleep apnoea, thickening of the uterine lining (which is why regular periods or protective treatment matter) and low mood or anxiety. Regular check-ups, even when you feel well, are part of treatment.

Questions answered

Frequently asked questions

Is PCOS curable?
PCOS cannot be cured, but its symptoms can be controlled very well with the right treatment and lifestyle changes. Many women have regular periods, clear skin and healthy pregnancies.
Can I get pregnant if I have PCOS?
Yes, most women with PCOS can. Some need medicines to help ovulation, and a few need IUI or other fertility treatment. Starting early with your doctor improves your chances.
Do I need to lose weight?
If you are overweight, even a modest weight loss can bring back regular periods and improve symptoms. Women with a normal weight can also have PCOS, and for them the focus is on other treatments and healthy habits.
Will I need medicines for life?
Not always. Some women do well with lifestyle changes alone, while others need medicines for certain periods, such as when they want to conceive. Your plan is reviewed regularly.
What tests are done for PCOS?
Usually a pelvic ultrasound and blood tests for hormones, thyroid, blood sugar and cholesterol. Your doctor decides which tests you need.
What is the difference between PCOD and PCOS?
The two names are often used for the same problem in everyday talk, while doctors use the term PCOS. What matters is a proper check-up to see how it affects you, and treatment for your symptoms and goals.
